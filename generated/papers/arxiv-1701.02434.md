## 主張

出典のリンクは原論文PDFの該当ページを開く。リンクにカーソルを合わせると原文の引用が表示される。

- **c1** Typical set: in high dimensions the neighbourhood around the mode has large density but little volume and the far tails have large volume but negligible density, so expectations are dominated by an intermediate region called the typical set, which becomes narrower (more singular) as dimension grows; the author presents this as a manifestation of concentration of measure.([Section 1.4, p.7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.02434v2#page=7 "The only signiﬁcant contributions come from the neighborhood between these two extremes known as the typical set (Figure 3)."))
- **c2** Random Walk Metropolis in high dimensions: almost every proposal lands outside the typical set and is rejected, while shrinking the proposal makes moves tiny; the author states that however it is tuned, the chain explores the typical set extremely slowly in all but the lowest dimensional spaces.([Section 2.4, p.16](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.02434v2#page=16 "Regardless of how we tune the covariance of the Random Walk Metropolis proposal or the particular details of the target distribution, the resulting Markov chain will explore the typical set extremely slowly in all but the lowest dimensional spaces."))
- **c3** Why momentum is needed: the gradient of the target density is parameterization-dependent and following it leads towards the mode rather than through the typical set; HMC adds auxiliary momentum with a probabilistic structure that gives conservative (Hamiltonian) dynamics, so trajectories glide along the typical set.([Section 3.1, p.19](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.02434v2#page=19 "Following the guidance of the gradient pulls us away from the typical set and towards the mode of the target density."))
- **c4** Pathologies and split R-hat: a typical set that pinches into a region of high curvature is missed by most Markov transitions, causing biased and oscillating estimators; the sufficient condition for a CLT is geometric ergodicity, which is hard to verify, so empirical diagnostics are used; the author calls split R-hat (across chains started from different points) the most powerful, and says values not near 1 should raise suspicion.([Section 2.3, p.15](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.02434v2#page=15 "Consequently, when split ˆR is not near the nominal value of 1, we should be suspicious of geometric ergodicity being satisﬁed and hence the practical utility of any resulting estimators."))
- **c5** Integration time: integrating too briefly wastes HMC's coherent exploration and integrating too long gives diminishing returns; the optimal time differs between trajectories (in a one-dimensional family it grows into the tails for heavy-tailed targets), so the author argues for choosing it dynamically, e.g. with the No-U-Turn criterion, which has proven an empirical success though near-optimality is shown only in some simple cases.([Section 4.3, p.34](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.02434v2#page=34 "In general, this optimal integration time will vary strongly depending on which trajectory we are considering: no single integration time will perform well everywhere."))
- **c6** Step size: for the simple static implementation, bounds between cost and average acceptance probability (citing Betancourt, Byrne and Girolami, 2014) underlie an adaptation that tunes the step size during an extended warm-up to an average Metropolis acceptance probability between 0.6 and 0.8; the author notes that more work is needed to formalize the corresponding results for more sophisticated implementations.([Section 5.3, p.41](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.02434v2#page=41 "These bounds provide the basis for an automated tuning algorithm that adapts the step size during an extended warm-up to achieve an average Metropolis acceptance probability between 0.6 and 0.8"))
- **c7** Divergences: regions of high curvature (characteristic of many models, in particular hierarchical models, citing Betancourt and Girolami, 2015) make symplectic integrators unstable so that trajectories diverge; any divergent transition should prompt suspicion of the estimators. Mild cases can be fixed by decreasing the step size, but often divergences persist however small the step, indicating significant bias, and the target itself must be regularized (stronger priors or an alternative representation such as switching centered/non-centered parameterizations).([Section 6.2, p.45](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.02434v2#page=45 "In particular, any divergent transitions encountered in a Hamiltonian Markov chain should prompt suspicion of the validity of any Markov chain Monte Carlo estimators."))
- **c8** Heavy tails and the kinetic energy: a poorly chosen kinetic energy can make the marginal energy distribution heavy-tailed so that exploration between energy level sets is incomplete; this is identified by comparing the marginal energy and energy-transition distributions, formalized as the energy Bayesian fraction of missing information (E-BFMI), with values below 0.3 empirically problematic but no formal threshold.([Section 6.1, p.44](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.02434v2#page=44 "Empirically, values of this energy Bayesian fraction of missing information below 0.3 have proven problematic"))
- **c9** Limits of the diagnostics: the E-BFMI and divergence diagnostics are only necessary, not sufficient, conditions for valid estimators; without explicit theoretical analysis there is no guarantee that a target will not have subtler pathologies, although the author argues that combined with split R-hat over multiple chains they make HMC uniquely robust.([Section 6.3, p.45](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.02434v2#page=45 "Although these two diagnostics are powerful means of identifying many pathological target distributions, they are only necessary and not suﬃcient conditions for the validity of the Markov chain Monte Carlo estimators."))
- **c10** Metropolis-Hastings: a proposal Q(q'|q) followed by a correction; the proposal is accepted with probability a(q'|q) = min(1, Q(q|q') pi(q') / (Q(q'|q) pi(q))).([Section 2.4, p.15](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.02434v2#page=15 "The Metropolis-Hastings algorithm is comprised of two steps: a proposal and a correction."))
- **c11** Random Walk Metropolis uses a Gaussian proposal N(q'|q, Sigma); because it is symmetric, the proposal density cancels and the acceptance probability becomes min(1, pi(q')/pi(q)).([Section 2.4, p.16](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.02434v2#page=16 "density cancels in the acceptance probability, leaving the simple form"))
- **c12** Hamiltonian: pi(q, p) = exp(-H(q, p)), and H(q, p) = -log pi(p|q) - log pi(q) = K(p, q) + V(q), the kinetic energy (density of the auxiliary momentum, specified by the implementation) plus the potential energy (determined by the target distribution); the value of H at a point is called its energy.([Section 3.2, p.25](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.02434v2#page=25 "Appealing to the physical analogy, the value of the Hamiltonian at any point in phase space is called the energy at that point."))
- **c13** Hamilton's equations: dq/dt = dH/dp = dK/dp and dp/dt = -dH/dq = -dK/dq - dV/dq; the gradient of the log target density enters through the momentum rather than moving the parameters directly.([Section 3.2, p.25](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.02434v2#page=25 "Recognizing ∂V/∂q as the gradient of the logarithm of the target density, we see that Hamilton’s equations fulﬁll exactly the intuition introduced in Section 3.1."))
- **c14** Leapfrog integrator (momentum distribution independent of position): with step size epsilon, repeat p_{n+1/2} = p_n - (epsilon/2) dV/dq(q_n), q_{n+1} = q_n + epsilon p_{n+1/2}, p_{n+1} = p_{n+1/2} - (epsilon/2) dV/dq(q_{n+1}); this interleaving ensures exact volume preservation on phase space.([Section 5.1, p.37](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.02434v2#page=37 "Given a time discretization, or step size, ϵ, the leapfrog integrator simulates the exact trajectory as"))
