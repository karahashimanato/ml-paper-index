## 主張

出典のリンクは原論文PDFの該当ページを開く。リンクにカーソルを合わせると原文の引用が表示される。

- **c1** Reward-model objective: following Christiano et al. (2017), labelers pick the best of four samples for an input; the reward model is fit by the softmax log-likelihood of the chosen sample among the four (Equation 1), is initialized as a random linear head on the language model, and is normalized to mean 0 and variance 1 on samples from the pretrained model. Asking for four options lets a human amortize the cost of reading the prompt.([Section 2, p.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.08593v2#page=3 "considering more options allows a human to amortize the cost of reading and understanding the prompt x."))
- **c2** RL objective: following Jaques et al. (2017; 2019), the policy is optimized with PPO on the modified reward R(x, y) = r(x, y) - beta log(pi(y|x)/rho(y|x)), a penalty with expectation beta KL(pi, rho) to the pretrained model rho. The authors state the term acts as an entropy bonus, keeps the policy within the range where r is valid, and for the style tasks is part of the task definition (style judged by humans, coherence and topicality left to the KL term). Beta is either constant or adapted by a log-space proportional controller toward a target KL (K_beta = 0.1).([Section 2, p.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.08593v2#page=3 "it plays the role of an entropy bonus, it prevents the policy from moving too far from the range where r is valid"))
- **c3** Without the KL penalty (mock sentiment task), samples are gibberish even with an entropy bonus, although the policies reach a very high mock-sentiment score.([Table 10 caption (appendix), p.18](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.08593v2#page=18 "Without regularization towards natural language, the results are gibberish even if we include an entropy bonus (targeting 30 nats)."))
- **c4** For the mock sentiment reward, the authors state that under a KL constraint to rho the optimal policy has the form pi_opt(y|x) proportional to rho(y|x) exp(r_s(x,y)/beta), and estimate its reward by reweighting samples from rho; their PPO policies show a significant gap from this optimum after the 2M training episodes used in the main experiments, largely closed with more training.([Section 3.1.1, p.6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.08593v2#page=6 "There is a signiﬁcant gap from optimality after training the policy on 2M continuations—the number used in our main experiments—though it is largely closed with more training."))
- **c5** Labelers and agreement: labels were collected through Scale AI using written instructions plus about 100 example comparisons from the authors. The queries lack unambiguous ground truth; on 4-way comparisons the authors agreed with each other about 60% of the time (25% by chance), and agreed with Scale labelers 38% of the time on sentiment and 46% on TL;DR summarization.([Section 2.4, p.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.08593v2#page=4 "On 4-way comparisons for sentiment and TL;DR summarization, authors of this paper agree about 60% of the time (vs. 25% for random guessing)."))
- **c6** Evaluation caveat: final evaluation uses comparisons by the same Scale labelers; the authors call this perilous because it shows that the reward model and policy fit the human reward but not that those evaluations capture what is really wanted, and the models are incentivized to exploit idiosyncrasies of the labeling process. They include samples so readers can judge.([Section 2.4, p.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.08593v2#page=4 "Evaluating the quality of a model trained by Scale using the same set of humans from Scale is perilous"))
- **c7** Distribution shift motivates online data collection: if the trained policy differs greatly from the pretrained policy, the reward model faces a large shift from its training samples; the authors report online collection was important for summarization but not for the simpler style tasks. Section 4.1 lists its disadvantages (software complexity, hard debugging, quality-control problems) and proposes batched data collection as a middle ground.([Section 2.3, p.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.08593v2#page=4 "If the trained policy π is very different from the zero-shot policy ρ, the reward model will suffer a large distributional shift from training on samples from ρ to evaluation on samples from π."))
- **c8** Summarization models trained with 60k labels are 'smart copiers' that mostly copy whole sentences while skipping preamble, yet the 60k online model beat the reference summaries 96% of the time on TL;DR and 84% on CNN/Daily Mail. The authors' interpretations: copying is the easiest way to be accurate since labelers were told to penalize inaccuracy and redundancy but not copying; and the preference for the model and lead-3 over references reveals a mismatch between the intended notion of quality and what labelers evaluated, which they failed to detect because online collection made quality control harder (the abstract says the models 'may be exploiting' labelers' simple heuristics).([Section 3.2.1, p.11](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.08593v2#page=11 "This reveals a mismatch between the notion of quality we wanted our model to learn, and what the humans labelers actually evaluated."))
- **c9** A refactoring bug flipped the sign of both the reward and the KL penalty, producing a model that optimized for negative sentiment while staying natural; because instructions told labelers to rate sexually explicit text very low, the model learned to output only such content, which was noticed only after training finished.([Section 4.4, p.12](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.08593v2#page=12 "This bug was remarkable since the result was not gibberish but maximally bad output."))
