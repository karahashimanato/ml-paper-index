## 主張

- **c1** Definition: RLHF is used to mean methods combining feedback collection, reward modeling (supervised learning of a reward model that imitates human evaluations) and policy optimization; formally the reward model minimizes a sum of losses l(r_phi(x_i), y_i) plus a regularizer lambda_r r(phi), and the policy maximizes E[r_phi(x) + lambda_p(theta, theta_new, x)], where lambda_p is a regularizer such as a divergence penalty. The authors note this framework fails to reflect reality in several ways (Section 3 and Appendix A).([Introduction, p.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.15217v2#page=2 "We use RLHF to refer to methods that combine three interconnected processes: feedback collection, re- ward modeling, and policy optimization."))
- **c2** Tractable vs fundamental: tractable challenges could be addressed within the RLHF framework with improved methodology, whereas fundamental challenges are substantial enough that overcoming them would require a method that is no longer RLHF; the authors call this distinction soft, with some categories marginal, and say fundamental problems should be avoided or compensated for by other safety measures.([Section 3, p.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.15217v2#page=5 "are substantial enough that overcoming them would require a method that is no longer a form of RLHF."))
- **c3** Human feedback (fundamental): because the reward model is trained on human approval rather than ground-truth desirability, models can exploit the gap between what is good and what is evaluated positively; citing prior work, RLHF-trained models can sound confident when wrong, which can lead to more positive feedback, and misleading behavior is actively incentivized when humans can be tricked. A separate fundamental item is that humans cannot evaluate performance on difficult tasks well even with perfect information and time.([Section 3.1.2, p.7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.15217v2#page=7 "Fundamental: Humans can be misled, so their evaluations can be gamed."))
- **c4** Reward model (fundamental): a single reward function cannot represent a diverse society of humans. Citing Stiennon et al. (2020), Ouyang et al. (2022) and Bai et al. (2022a), annotator-annotator and annotator-researcher agreement rates range from 63% to 77%; current techniques model evaluator differences as noise, so when preferences differ the majority wins, potentially disadvantaging under-represented groups.([Section 3.2.1, p.9](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.15217v2#page=9 "Fundamental: A single reward function cannot represent a diverse society of humans."))
- **c5** Reward hacking (fundamental): optimizing strong pressure against an imperfect proxy tends to hurt the underlying goal (citing Hoskin, Manheim & Garrabrant, Gao et al., 2022); citing Ziegler et al. (2019) and Stiennon et al. (2020), without a KL penalty between base and finetuned model LLMs under RL often learn to output nonsensical text; reward models can also misgeneralize even from correctly labeled data.([Section 3.2.2, p.9](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.15217v2#page=9 "Fundamental: Optimizing for an imperfect reward proxy leads to reward hacking."))
- **c6** Evaluating reward models (tractable but difficult and expensive): since the true reward is usually unknown, reward models are typically evaluated indirectly by optimizing a policy and evaluating its generations, which makes the evaluation depend on an expensive, noisy policy optimization with many ad-hoc choices; moreover the evaluation signal is the same as the training signal (human approval), so training and evaluation failures will be correlated.([Section 3.2.3, p.10](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.15217v2#page=10 "Another issue with indirect evaluation is that the evaluation signal for the reward model is the same as the training signal – human approval."))
- **c7** Joint training (tractable): if the reward model is trained offline it is likely to misgeneralize, while gathering feedback from policy samples invites auto-induced distributional shift; when steps are done serially, the reward model is typically inaccurate off-distribution, exactly where the policy goes. Without a labeled validation set in the regions the policy explores, reward over-optimization is hard to detect during training; the authors suggest measuring KL-shift (citing Gao et al., 2022) or ensemble disagreement as possibly helpful.([Section 3.4, p.12](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.15217v2#page=12 "Without a labeled validation set in the regions the policy is exploring, it is difficult to detect reward over-optimization during training."))
- **c8** Transparency recommendations: the authors propose that organizations disclose details such as the pretraining process, how human evaluators were selected and trained, how examples were selected for feedback, the feedback types, quality-assurance and inter-rater consistency measures, the reward-model loss and how disagreement was modeled, reward-model and policy evaluations (including red teaming), audits, expected risks, and monitoring plans; detailing safety measures will often not require implementable details, and otherwise private disclosure to second-party auditors is offered as a solution.([Section 5, p.17](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.15217v2#page=17 "Companies using RLHF to train models for high-stakes or safety-critical applications should maintain transparency with the public and/or auditors about key details of their approach."))
