## 主張

出典のリンクは原論文PDFの該当ページを開く。リンクにカーソルを合わせると原文の引用が表示される。

- **c1** Conformal validity relies on two assumptions: exchangeability of the data and a model-fitting algorithm that treats data points symmetrically. The paper drops both, generalizing split conformal, full conformal and the jackknife+.([Abstract, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.13415v5#page=1 "Its validity relies on the assumptions of exchangeability of the data, and symmetry of the given model ﬁtting algorithm as a function of the data."))
- **c2** What the guarantee becomes: the coverage gap ((1 - alpha) minus the actual coverage) is bounded by a weighted sum of total-variation distances between the data (or, more tightly, the residual vectors) and the same with the test point swapped with point i, with no assumption on the joint distribution. The weights must be fixed (not data-dependent), and the authors state that using these methods implicitly assumes this weighted TV sum is small.([Introduction, p.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.13415v5#page=4 "In particular, if we use these methods, we are implicitly assuming that this weighted sum of total variation terms is small."))
- **c3** Under exchangeability the bound is zero, so introducing fixed weights loses no coverage; with unit weights and a symmetric algorithm the theorems also give a new robustness result for standard split/full conformal (gap at most sum_i d_TV / (n + 1)), which the authors say helps explain their success on real data where exchangeability may not hold. Theorem 3 gives an upper bound on coverage (no substantial overcoverage) when w~_{n+1} is small.([Theory (Remarks on the theorems), p.17](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.13415v5#page=17 "These new bounds ensure robustness of existing methods against mild violations of the exchangeability (or i.i.d.) assumption, and thus help explain the success of these methods on real data, where the exchangeability assumption may not hold."))
- **c4** Cost of weighting: low weights reduce the effective sample size (e.g. for estimating the residual quantile), so overly low weights often widen intervals; all-zero weights give zero coverage gap but an uninformative interval (the whole real line). Optimal weight choice is left open.([Theory (Remarks on the theorems), p.18](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.13415v5#page=18 "How to choose weights optimally (and, even how to quantify optimality) is an interesting and important question that we leave for future work."))
- **c5** When the guarantee is useful: the bound holds with no distributional assumption but is meaningless if large, so the weights should be chosen in advance from (at least qualitative) prior knowledge of the likely deviation, e.g. geometric decay under gradual drift; if the test point comes from a distribution bearing no resemblance to the training data, no method can guarantee coverage without further assumptions. Whether coverage will likely hold for a particular data set is named as an open question.([Theory (Is the guarantee useful?), p.18](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.13415v5#page=18 "On the other hand, if the test point comes from a new distribution that bears no resemblance to the training data, neither our bound nor any other method would be able to guarantee valid coverage without further assumptions."))
- **c6** Examples with weights w_i = rho^(n+1-i): for independent data with Lipschitz-type bounded drift (d_TV(Z_i, Z_{n+1}) <= eps (n + 1 - i)) the gap is at most 2 eps / (1 - rho); after a changepoint k steps ago the gap is at most rho^k, small only when plenty of data has been observed since the changepoint.([Section 4.4 (Examples), p.19](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.13415v5#page=19 "This yields a small coverage gap as long as k is large, i.e., as long as we have plenty of data observed after the most recent changepoint."))
- **c7** Nonsymmetric algorithms need the random swap step for the guarantee; the swap may alter the fitted model and could make predictions far less accurate in some settings (e.g. autoregressive models where the most recent point matters most). The theory does not rely on stability assumptions, and practical implementation in such settings is left to future work.([Section 3 (The swap step), p.15](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.13415v5#page=15 "We leave the important question of practical implementation for such settings, and the question of how to choose algorithms A that will not be too sensitive to the swap, to future work."))
- **c8** Contrast with weighted conformal under covariate shift (citing Tibshirani et al. 2019): that method needs the covariate-shift assumption and a known or well-approximated, possibly high-dimensional likelihood ratio, with data-dependent weights; here the weights are fixed and can compensate for unknown but small violations. The authors state that if the data were in fact exchangeable, the covariate-shift weighted algorithm has no coverage guarantee, while theirs retains exact coverage.([Section 2 (Further related work), p.8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.13415v5#page=8 "Finally, and importantly, if there was actually no distribution shift, and the data happened to be exchangeable, their weighted algorithm does not have any coverage guarantee, while ours retains exact coverage."))
- **c9** Simulations (N = 2000, Gaussian covariates; Setting 1 i.i.d., Setting 2 two changepoints in the coefficients, Setting 3 linear drift; online after a 100-point burn-in, 200 replications, target 90%): full conformal with least squares (CP+LS) undercovers after changepoints and as drift grows, while NexCP+LS and NexCP+WLS (weights and tags 0.99^(n+1-i)) better maintain coverage; NexCP+WLS gives narrower intervals than CP+LS, and weighted methods have slightly higher width variability under i.i.d. data (lower effective sample size).([Section 5.1 (Simulations), p.24](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.13415v5#page=24 "The NexCP+LS and NexCP+WLS methods are better able to maintain coverage in these settings."))
