## 主張

出典のリンクは原論文PDFの該当ページを開く。リンクにカーソルを合わせると原文の引用が表示される。

- **c1** Core idea: since noisy solutions to moment equations are generally biased and averaging per-tree solutions would not remove the bias, GRF treats the forest as an adaptive locally weighted estimator: forest weights alpha_i(x) (how often training example i shares a leaf with x) replace classical kernel weights, and the estimating equation is solved with these weights. For conditional means this is equivalent to Breiman's averaging.([Introduction, p.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1610.01271v4#page=2 "To avoid this issue, we cast forests as a type of adaptive locally weighted estimators that ﬁrst use a forest to calculate a weighted set of neighbors for each test point x, and then solve a plug-in version of the estimating equation (1) using these neighbors."))
- **c2** Splitting rule: splits aim to maximise heterogeneity of the in-sample theta estimates between children (Delta criterion, motivated by Proposition 1); to avoid solving the estimating equation for every candidate split, a labeling step computes gradient-based pseudo-outcomes (influence-function values at the parent estimate) and a regression step runs a standard CART split on them, so existing optimized tree software (ranger) can be used. For least-squares regression this recovers the usual regression-forest split.([Section 2.3, p.9](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1610.01271v4#page=9 "Next, in a regression step, we run a standard CART regression split on the pseudo-outcomes ρi."))
- **c3** Forest requirements for the theory (Specification 1): trees are symmetric, make balanced splits (each child gets at least a fraction omega of the parent), split on each feature with probability at least pi (implemented by trying min{max{Poisson(m),1},p} variables), and the forest is honest (Algorithm 1 splits each subsample into halves, one to grow the tree and one to populate the leaves) and built by subsampling without replacement.([Section 3, p.13](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1610.01271v4#page=13 "The forest is honest and built via subsampling with subsample size s satisfying s/n →0 and s →∞, as described in Section 2.4."))
- **c4** Asymptotic results: given Assumptions 1-6 (Lipschitz x-signal, smooth identification, Lipschitz variogram, regularity of psi, existence of solutions, convexity) and a forest per Specification 1, the estimates are consistent (Theorem 3); with subsample size s = n^beta for beta between a beta_min and 1 and positive conditional variance of the influence function, they are asymptotically Gaussian (Theorem 5). The nuisance parameters are also consistent and asymptotically normal, but the authors caution they may be inaccurate in finite samples because splits are not targeted at them.([Section 3.1, p.16](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1610.01271v4#page=16 "however, we caution that the tree splits are not necessarily targeted to expressing heterogeneity in ν(x), and so the resulting ˆν(x) may not be particularly accurate in ﬁnite samples."))
- **c5** Confidence intervals: delta-method intervals use a problem-specific curvature estimate and an estimate of the forest score variance from a variant of the bootstrap of little bags (Sexton and Laake, 2009), where trees are grown in little bags sharing a half-sample; Theorem 6 states the half-sampling variance estimator is consistent and the intervals asymptotically have nominal coverage given a consistent curvature estimate. With few trees the variance estimate can be negative, which the software handles with a Bayesian analysis of variance.([Section 4, p.17](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1610.01271v4#page=17 "As a side beneﬁt, we also obtain the ﬁrst consistency guarantees for this method for any type of forest, including regression forests."))
- **c6** Causal forests in GRF: for conditional average partial / treatment effects the regressors W are assumed exogenous given X (for binary W, equivalent to unconfoundedness); performance can be improved by locally centering Y and W with leave-one-out estimates of E[Y|X] and E[W|X] (Robinson-style orthogonalization). The theory covers deterministic centering or k-fold cross-fitting, not leave-one-out centering exactly.([Section 6.1.1, p.22](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1610.01271v4#page=22 "however, a practitioner wanting to use results that are precisely covered by theory may prefer to use cross-ﬁtting for centering."))
- **c7** Stated limitations: the confidence intervals rely on undersmoothing for valid asymptotic coverage and do not capture bias otherwise, so a principled bias correction is left open; forests can also exhibit edge effects where the slope of the estimates tapers off near the boundary of X-space.([Section 8 (Discussion), p.27](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1610.01271v4#page=27 "In particular, as discussed above, our conﬁdence interval construction relies on undersmoothing to get valid asymptotic coverage (without undersmoothing, the conﬁdence intervals account for sampling variability of the forest, but do not capture bias)."))
- **c8** Coverage check for instrumental-variables forests (simulations with confounding and nuisance terms, centered forests, B = 4,000 trees, 20 replications): coverage is closer to nominal when the effect depends on 2 features and declines considerably with 4 features at the sample sizes studied; comparing with coverage of the expected forest prediction, the authors suggest the shortfall is mostly due to non-negligible bias.([Appendix C.3, p.48](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1610.01271v4#page=48 "These results suggest that low coverage numbers in the left panel are mostly due to our forests having non-negligible bias, rather than to failures of Gaussianity or of the variance estimates underlying our conﬁdence intervals."))
