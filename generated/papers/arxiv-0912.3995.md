## 主張

出典のリンクは原論文PDFの該当ページを開く。リンクにカーソルを合わせると原文の引用が表示される。

- **c1** Setting: optimizing an unknown, noisy, expensive function is cast as a multi-armed bandit where the payoff function is either sampled from a GP or has low RKHS norm; performance is measured by cumulative regret (sum of f(x*) - f(x_t)), and bounds on average regret translate into convergence rates.([Abstract, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0912.3995v4#page=1 "We formalize this task as a multi-armed bandit problem, where the payoﬀfunction is either sampled from a Gaussian process (GP) or has low RKHS norm."))
- **c2** Motivation for the UCB rule: picking the point of maximum posterior mean is too greedy too soon and tends to get stuck in shallow local optima, while the experimental-design rule (maximum variance) reduces uncertainty globally rather than where maxima might be; GP-UCB combines the mean with beta_t^{1/2} times the standard deviation.([Section 3, p.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0912.3995v4#page=4 "However, this rule is too greedy too soon and tends to get stuck in shallow local optima."))
- **c3** Theorem 1 (finite domain): if f is a sample from a zero-mean GP with known covariance k (k(x,x) <= 1), observed with i.i.d. Gaussian noise of variance sigma^2, and beta_t = 2 log(|D| t^2 pi^2 / 6 delta), then with probability at least 1 - delta the cumulative regret is at most sqrt(C1 T beta_T gamma_T) for all T, with C1 = 8 / log(1 + sigma^-2).([Section 4, p.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0912.3995v4#page=5 "This theorem shows that, with high probability over samples from the GP, the cumulative regret is bounded in terms of the maximum information gain, forging a novel connection between GP optimization and experimental design."))
- **c4** Theorem 2 (compact convex D in [0, r]^d) additionally assumes a high-probability bound on the derivatives of GP sample paths and uses a beta_t that grows with an extra 2d log term. This holds for stationary kernels that are four times differentiable, such as squared exponential and Matern with nu > 2, but is violated for the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck kernel, for which the authors conjecture a result of this form does not hold.([Section 4, p.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0912.3995v4#page=5 "The smoothness assumption on k(x, x′) disqualiﬁes GPs with highly erratic sample paths."))
- **c5** Theorem 3 (agnostic case): if f lies in the RKHS of k with squared norm at most B and the noise is a martingale difference sequence bounded by sigma, GP-UCB run with a GP(0, k) prior and Gaussian noise model (both misspecified) and beta_t = 2B + 300 gamma_t log^3(t/delta) still attains sublinear regret with high probability over the noise. It needs an upper bound on the RKHS norm (guess-and-doubling is suggested if unknown); GP samples have infinite RKHS norm almost surely, so neither Theorem 2 nor 3 encompasses the other.([Section 4, p.6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0912.3995v4#page=6 "Note that we still run the same GP-UCB algorithm, whose prior and noise model are misspeciﬁed in this case."))
- **c6** Information gain bounds (Theorem 5, compact convex D, k(x,x) <= 1): gamma_T = O(d log T) for linear kernels, O((log T)^{d+1}) for the squared exponential, and a power law in T for Matern kernels with nu > 1; together with Theorems 2 and 3 this gives sublinear regret in any dimension. For the squared exponential, d enters only as the exponent of log T, which the authors attribute to the high smoothness of its sample paths.([Section 5.2, p.7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0912.3995v4#page=7 "the high degree of smoothness of the sample paths eﬀectively combats the curse of dimensionality"))
- **c7** beta in practice vs theory: on the synthetic experiments (functions sampled from a squared exponential kernel with lengthscale 0.2, D = [0, 1] discretized into 1000 points, noise variance 0.025, T = 1000, delta = 0.1, 30 trials), the Theorem 1 beta_t gives competitive performance, but scaling beta_t down by a factor of 5, found by cross-validation, improves the algorithm; the authors note they did not optimize the constants in the bounds.([Section 6, p.7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0912.3995v4#page=7 "While the choice of βt as recommended by Theorem 1 leads to competitive performance of GP-UCB, we ﬁnd (using cross-validation) that the algorithm is improved by scaling βt down by a factor 5."))
- **c8** Sensor experiments: the kernel matrix is the empirical covariance computed from the first two-thirds of the data, and objectives come from the remaining third (temperature: 46 sensors, T = 46, averaged over 2000 objectives; traffic: 357 sensors, T = 357, 900 runs; 5% noise, delta = 0.1). GP-UCB is compared with EI, MPI, mean-only and variance-only rules: on temperature data GP-UCB and EI clearly outperform the others without significant difference between them, and on synthetic and traffic data MPI does equally well.([Section 6, p.8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0912.3995v4#page=8 "In summary, GP-UCB performs at least on par with the existing approaches which are not equipped with regret bounds."))
- **c9** GP posterior with noisy observations (Eq. 1-2): for y_t = f(x_t) + eps_t with i.i.d. N(0, sigma^2) noise, the posterior over f is again a GP with mean mu_T(x) = k_T(x)^T (K_T + sigma^2 I)^-1 y_T, covariance k_T(x, x') = k(x, x') - k_T(x)^T (K_T + sigma^2 I)^-1 k_T(x'), and variance sigma_T^2(x) = k_T(x, x), where k_T(x) = [k(x_1, x) ... k(x_T, x)]^T and K_T is the kernel matrix.([Section 2, p.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0912.3995v4#page=3 "A major advantage of working with GPs is the existence of simple analytic formulae for mean and covariance of the posterior distribution, which allows easy implementation of algorithms."))
- **c10** GP-UCB rule (Eq. 6): x_t = argmax_{x in D} mu_{t-1}(x) + beta_t^{1/2} sigma_{t-1}(x), preferring both uncertain points (large sigma) and points with high expected reward (large mu), which implicitly negotiates the exploration-exploitation tradeoff; beta_t is specified depending on the context.([Section 3, p.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0912.3995v4#page=4 "it implicitly negotiates the exploration–exploitation tradeoﬀ."))
