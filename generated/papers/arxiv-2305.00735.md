- **c1** No hyperparameter optimization: each algorithm is run with several sensible hyperparameter settings per dataset and the ROC-AUC is averaged over them; hyperparameters are not optimized (e.g. by cross-validation) to reflect that no labels are available in practice.([Section 3 (Materials and Methods), p.6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.00735v1#page=6 "We refrain from optimizing hyperparameters, e.g., using cross-validation, to reﬂect the real-world situation in which no labels are available for training the models."))
- **c2** Implementations: 27 of the 32 methods are used as implemented in PyOD, and as part of this research the authors contributed to PyOD (a memory-efficient COF and the Birgé-Rozenholc histogram size selection used in HBOS and LODA); EIF uses the authors' 'eif' package, ODIN is implemented by the authors, and DeepSVDD uses Lukas Ruff's public code modified for general tabular datasets.([Section 3.1, p.6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.00735v1#page=6 "As part of this research, we made several contributions to this open source library"))
- **c3** Evaluation: anomaly scores are computed on all available data at once, without cross-validation or train-test splits, and compared with the ground-truth labels by ROC AUC; R-precision, average precision and their chance-adjusted variants were considered but found less stable and harder to interpret. Differences are tested with the Iman-Davenport statistic and the Nemenyi post-hoc test.([Section 3.3, p.10](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.00735v1#page=10 "For each dataset, we calculate anomaly scores on all available data at once, without using any cross-validation or train-test splits."))
- **c4** Dataset handling: duplicates are removed and variables are centered by the median and scaled by the interquartile range; three datasets on which nearly every algorithm had AUC between 0.4 and 0.6 were excluded, and for two datasets (skin, vertebral) where the more heterogeneous group had been chosen as the normal class, labels were inverted and AUC recalculated.([Section 4.1, p.10](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.00735v1#page=10 "These datasets, the ‘hrss anomalous standard’, ‘wpbc’ and ‘yeast’ dataset, were therefore excluded from further analysis."))
- **c5** Overall: kth-NN significantly outperforms 14/15 algorithms at p = 0.05/0.10, and Isolation Forest and its extended version show similarly consistent performance; since their cost scales linearly with the number of samples, this may give them a clear edge over kNN-type methods on large datasets. The original CBLOF is by far the worst.([Section 4.1, p.13](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.00735v1#page=13 "Since the computational complexity of Isolation Forest and variants thereof scales linearly with the number of samples n, this may give them a clear edge over methods such as kNN and derivatives for large datasets"))
- **c6** Neural networks: many of them do not perform well ((soft-boundary) DeepSVDD, ALAD and SO-GAAL are in the lower segment). The authors surmise two reasons: they were not designed for tabular data and cannot leverage the feature extraction that helps them in computer vision, and they are complex, making general hyperparameters and architectures hard to specify. The autoencoder and its variants and LUNAR perform about average, likely due to more straightforward optimisation criteria.([Section 4.1, p.13](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.00735v1#page=13 "From these overall results it is clear that many of the neural networks do not perform well."))
- **c7** Local vs global: clustering the results splits the datasets into roughly a quarter 'local' and three quarters 'global' problems. Local methods (COF, ensemble-LOF, LOF) are among the worst overall but among the best on the local datasets, which the authors call an example of Simpson's paradox and a partial explanation of the difference from Campos et al. (2016). On local datasets kNN and ABOD are the top performers; on global datasets the Extended Isolation Forest has the highest mean, closely followed by Isolation Forest.([Section 4.3, p.15](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.00735v1#page=15 "This phenomenon is a ﬁne example of Simpson’s paradox (Simpson, 1951)."))
- **c8** Conclusion: the authors are 'tempted' to answer 'three': k-thNN, kNN and EIF seem sufficient for the type of multivariate datasets in the study (k-thNN without prior knowledge of anomaly type, kNN for local, EIF/IF for global, with IF/EIF having the lowest computational complexity). The conclusion is stated as open to revision when other algorithms or datasets are added; raw images, texts and time series are left to future work.([Section 6 (Conclusion), p.21](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.00735v1#page=21 "a toolbox with k-thNN, kNN, and EIF seems suﬃcient to perform well on the type of multivariate datasets considered in our study"))
