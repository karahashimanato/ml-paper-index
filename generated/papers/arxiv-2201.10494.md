## 主張

出典のリンクは原論文PDFの該当ページを開く。リンクにカーソルを合わせると原文の引用が表示される。

- **c1** Main finding on Li et al. (2018), per Boether et al.: after re-implementing Li et al.'s algorithm and testing various configurations on small and large synthetic and real-world MIS graphs, they report that the GCN in the guided tree search does not learn a meaningful representation of the solution structure and can be replaced by random values; they state the tree search instead relies on algorithmic techniques like graph kernelization and conclude that the original publication's results are not reproducible.([Abstract, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.10494v1#page=1 "By re-implementing their algorithm with a focus on code quality and extensibility, we show that the graph convolution network used in the tree search does not learn a meaningful representation of the solution structure, and can in fact be replaced by random values."))
- **c2** Attribution of performance (Boether et al.'s conclusion): the GCN guidance does not help their tree search (randomness beats the GCN on some datasets, no difference on others); in what they consider the production configuration (reduction and local search enabled) random outputs give identical performance with very minor differences in time to the final solution; overall they name KaMIS's reduction and local search as the major contributors to solution quality and call the tree search a 'smarter brute-force' approach that does not gain performance from ML guidance (they recall the default version cannot solve SATLIB without reduction and local search).([Analysis of the tree search, p.8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.10494v1#page=8 "Overall, major contributing factors to solution quality are the reduction and local search by KAMIS, and in fact, the tree search algorithm is a “smarter brute-force” approach that does not gain any performance by being guided by a machine learning model."))
- **c3** Reproducibility (Boether et al.): on SATLIB, with the default configuration (no reduction, no local search) and their time limits (30 s for SATLIB in Tables 1/2), both implementations rarely find solutions; they state their results contradict Li et al.'s claimed SATLIB average MIS of 426 and Ahn et al. (2020)'s 418, that testing the official Intel-TreeSearch weights made no difference, and that neither the original code with the original weights, nor the original code with newly trained weights, nor their re-implementation perform as originally claimed; they contacted Li et al. unsuccessfully (their suspicion of an undocumented modification refers to Ahn et al.'s default-configuration results).([Analysis of the tree search, p.7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.10494v1#page=7 "Overall, neither the original code with the original weights, nor the original code with newly trained weights, nor our reimplementation are performing as originally claimed."))
- **c4** Undocumented implementation details of the original code (Appendix C, per Boether et al.): the multi-threaded variant uses just one randomly chosen probability map instead of all, which is not documented in the paper; also one 'epoch' iterates over only 2000 random samples of the 38000 training samples although the paper states 200 epochs.([Appendix, p.20](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.10494v1#page=20 "Third, the multi-threaded variant of the algorithm (demo parallel.py) uses just one randomly chosen probability map of the GCN outputs instead of all."))
- **c5** Comparison with classical solvers (Boether et al., on their main benchmark datasets before the separate large-scale analysis): they find state-of-the-art algorithmic techniques are required for the tree searches to find good solutions; because the fully configured tree searches use KaMIS-internal routines, they argue ('one can argue') purely algorithmic solvers are the better choice, as the quality difference is negligible and KaMIS is faster; they say this is in line with Joshi et al. (2021) (arxiv-2006.07054), who in their description observe DL-based TSP solutions are often outperformed, especially on larger instances.([Comparing tree search solvers to other solvers, p.8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.10494v1#page=8 "As the fully-conﬁgured versions of the tree searches employ these KAMIS-internal routines, one can argue that the purely algorithmic solvers are the better choice, because the quality difference is negligible, while KAMIS is faster than the tree searches."))
- **c6** Contrast with an RL solver (Boether et al.): for Learning What To Defer (Ahn et al. 2020), trained by them for 20,000 PPO iterations on SATLIB with Ahn et al.'s SATLIB hyperparameters and run without local search or reduction, replacing the network output with a random tensor degrades solution quality noticeably, unlike for the tree searches, so they conclude the GNN is responsible for its competitive solution quality.([Abstract, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.10494v1#page=1 "Additionally, we analyze a recent solver based on reinforcement learning and observe that for this solver, the GNN is responsible for the competitive solution quality."))
- **c7** Hardware fairness: Gurobi and KaMIS run on CPU while the tree search uses one or more GPUs; even with eight V100 GPUs the tree search does not outperform the other solvers, which the authors read as the workload not being GPU-bound.([Comparing tree search solvers to other solvers, p.8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.10494v1#page=8 "Evening when assigning eight V100 GPUs to the multithreaded tree search, the computational advantage of the tree search does not help to outperform the other solvers, showing that the workload is not GPU-bound."))
