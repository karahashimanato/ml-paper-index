## 主張

出典のリンクは原論文PDFの該当ページを開く。リンクにカーソルを合わせると原文の引用が表示される。

- **c1** GCN layer as described (citing Kipf & Welling 2017): the K-localized ChebNet filter is simplified by limiting K = 1 and approximating the largest eigenvalue of L_sym by 2, then the renormalization trick I + D^(-1/2) A D^(-1/2) -> D~^(-1/2) A~ D~^(-1/2) with A~ = A + I is applied, giving H^(l+1) = sigma(D~^(-1/2) A~ D~^(-1/2) H^(l) Theta^(l)); the semi-supervised model is a two-layer GCN with softmax output and cross-entropy over labeled vertices.([Section 2, p.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1801.07606v1#page=3 "In (Kipf and Welling 2017), Kipf and Welling simpliﬁed this model by limiting K = 1 and approximating the largest eigenvalue λmax of Lsym by 2."))
- **c2** Laplacian smoothing view: Laplacian smoothing (citing Taubin 1995) is y_i = (1 - gamma) x_i + gamma sum_j (a~_ij / d_i) x_j; with gamma = 1 and the symmetrically normalized Laplacian in place of D~^(-1) L~, it becomes exactly the GCN graph convolution D~^(-1/2) A~ D~^(-1/2) X, which the authors call symmetric Laplacian smoothing (still including the vertex's own features through the self-loop).([Section 3, p.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1801.07606v1#page=4 "We thus call the graph convolution a special form of Laplacian smoothing – symmetric Laplacian smoothing."))
- **c3** Why GCNs work: on Cora with 20 labels per class, the only difference between a GCN and a fully connected network is the graph convolution matrix, and even a one-layer GCN outperformed a one-layer FCN by a very large margin; the authors explain this by smoothing making features of densely connected same-cluster vertices similar, and attribute the further gain of a 2-layer GCN to applying smoothing again.([Section 3, p.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1801.07606v1#page=3 "Surprisingly, even a one-layer GCN outperformed a one-layer FCN by a very large margin."))
- **c4** When GCNs fail: more layers are 'certainly not the more the better' since a many-layer GCN is difficult to train and repeated Laplacian smoothing may mix features from different clusters; this is illustrated with untrained, randomly initialized GCNs of 1-5 layers on Zachary's karate club (34 vertices, 78 edges), where points become mixed after repeated smoothing (the authors note mixing happens quickly on this small dataset with many inter-class connections).([Section 3, p.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1801.07606v1#page=4 "On the other hand, repeatedly applying Laplacian smoothing may mix the features of vertices from different clusters and make them indistinguishable."))
- **c5** Theorem 1 (over-smoothing) and its assumptions: if a graph has no bipartite components, then for any w and alpha in (0, 1], (I - alpha L_rw)^m w and (I - alpha L_sym)^m w converge as m -> infinity to linear combinations of the connected-component indicator vectors and of D^(-1/2) times them, respectively; since the self-loops remove bipartite components, the authors conclude over-smoothing will make features indistinguishable and hurt accuracy. The theorem is stated for repeated application of the linear smoothing operator.([Section 3, p.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1801.07606v1#page=5 "Based on the above theorem, over-smoothing will make the features indistinguishable and hurt the classiﬁcation accuracy."))
- **c6** Shallow GCNs have the opposite limit: because the graph convolution is a localized filter, a shallow GCN cannot sufficiently propagate label information to the whole graph with few labels; in Fig. 1 GCN accuracy drops quickly as the training size shrinks and faster than label propagation, which the authors read as an inability to explore global graph structure.([Section 3, p.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1801.07606v1#page=5 "However, since the graph convolution is a localized ﬁlter – a linear combination of the feature vectors of adjacent neighbors, a shallow GCN cannot sufﬁciently propagate the label information to the entire graph with only a few labels."))
- **c7** Evaluation critique: the GCN of Kipf & Welling (2017) uses an additional 500 labeled vertices for early stopping, i.e. validation-set model selection, which is more than the total training labels; optimizing without validation causes a significant drop, and the authors argue this defeats the purpose of semi-supervised learning and makes comparisons with methods like label propagation unfair.([Section 3, p.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1801.07606v1#page=5 "Furthermore, it makes the comparison of GCNs with other methods unfair as other methods such as label propagation may not need the validation data at all."))
- **c8** Protocol: CiteSeer, Cora and PubMed (data from Yang et al. 2016 and Kipf & Welling 2017); GCN hyperparameters copied from Kipf & Welling (learning rate 0.01, 200 max epochs, dropout 0.5, L2 5e-4, 2 layers, 16 hidden units, validated on Cora by Kipf and Welling); each run randomly splits labels into a small training set and 1000 test samples; label rates 0.5-5% on Cora/CiteSeer and 0.03-0.3% on PubMed; mean accuracy of 50 runs except PubMed results from Yang et al., averaged over 10 runs. In the 20-labels-per-class comparison, other baselines' results are copied from Kipf & Welling.([Section 5, p.6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1801.07606v1#page=6 "For each run, we randomly split labels into a small set for training, and a set with 1000 samples for testing."))
- **c9** Depth in the experiments: following Kipf and Welling, 2 convolutional layers were used, 2-layer GCNs performed best, and accuracy decreased drastically as layers grew, which the authors attribute (with 'probably') to overfitting rather than to over-smoothing.([Section 5, p.7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1801.07606v1#page=7 "When the number of convolutional layers grows, the classiﬁcation accuracy decreases drastically, which is probably due to overﬁtting."))
