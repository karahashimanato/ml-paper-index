## 主張

出典のリンクは原論文PDFの該当ページを開く。リンクにカーソルを合わせると原文の引用が表示される。

- **c1** CASH can be reformulated as a single combined hierarchical hyperparameter optimization problem whose parameter space is the union of the algorithms' hyperparameter spaces plus a new root-level hyperparameter selecting among the algorithms; the root-level parameters of each subspace are conditional on that choice. The authors name Bayesian optimization, in particular sequential model-based optimization (SMBO), as a promising approach because it handles categorical and continuous hyperparameters and conditional structure.([Section 3, p.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1208.3719v2#page=2 "We note that this problem can be reformulated as a single combined hierarchical hyperparameter optimization problem"))
- **c2** SMAC (as used here, with random-forest models) instantiates inactive conditional parameters to defaults, uses expected improvement, and evaluates cross-validation folds one at a time: a new configuration must beat the incumbent on every comparison over the folds used so far, so a poor configuration can be dropped after a single fold; every second configuration is selected at random as a diversification safeguard.([Section 3.1, p.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1208.3719v2#page=3 "This means that a poorly performing conﬁguration can be discarded after considering as little as a single fold."))
- **c3** TPE models p(lambda | c) with two density estimates, l for configurations with loss below the gamma-quantile threshold (default gamma = 0.15) and g for the rest, and picks candidates with the smallest g/l among randomly generated ones; because of its tree-structured 1-D Parzen estimators it assumes independence for hyperparameters that never appear together on a root-to-leaf path.([Section 3.2, p.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1208.3719v2#page=3 "Note that this means that TPE assumes independence for hyperparameters that do not appear together along any path from the tree’s root to one of its leaves."))
- **c4** Search space: two top-level Booleans (base classifier vs. ensemble/meta classifier; feature selection on/off) with conditional choices below (meta-methods take one base classifier, ensembles up to five), giving four hierarchical layers and 786 parameters in total; the authors estimate that discretizing each domain to at most 10 values would give over 10^47 settings.([Section 4, p.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1208.3719v2#page=5 "This results in a very wide tree that captures all the hierarchical nature of the model hyperparameters, and allows the creation of a single hyperparameter optimization problem with four hierarchical layers of a total of 786 parameters."))
- **c5** Protocol: 21 datasets (15 UCI, convex, MNIST basic, rotated MNIST with background images, KDD Cup 09 appetency, CIFAR-10 and a 10,000-point subset); predefined splits or a random 70/30 train/test split, with test data used once offline. Runs on Intel Xeon X5650 machines with a 3GB RAM limit per classifier (exceeding it counts as 100% error), a per-fold training timeout of 150 minutes (15 for feature search/evaluation), and a 30-hour budget per optimizer; 25 seeded runs per method, with bootstrap sampling of 4 runs (keeping the best cross-validation run) to simulate a 4-core workstation.([Section 5.1, p.6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1208.3719v2#page=6 "For each method, we performed 25 runs of this process with diﬀerent random seeds and then – in order to simulate parallelization on a typical workstation – used bootstrap sampling to repeatedly select 4 random runs and report the performance of the one with best cross-validation performance."))
- **c6** Baselines: Ex-Def picks, by 10-fold cross-validation, the best of the 39 WEKA classifiers at default hyperparameters; Random Grid performs random search over the union of hyperparameter grids for the 27 base classifiers plus the 39 defaults, which the authors call a simple CASH algorithm. Random Grid used 400 CPU hours per dataset on average (at least 120), versus 4 x 30 CPU hours for Auto-WEKA. Best/worst 'oracle' columns are measured on the test set.([Section 5.2, p.6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1208.3719v2#page=6 "We executed this Random Grid search for all our datasets in parallel, using 400 CPU hours on average per dataset (at least 120 hours for each)."))
- **c7** On test data the differences were less pronounced than in cross-validation: the text reports Auto-WEKA outperforming Random Grid and Ex-Def in 15/21 cases with 3 ties and 3 losses, and Random Grid beating Ex-Def in only 9/21 cases. Auto-WEKA performed best on all 11 largest datasets, which the authors attribute to overfitting risk decreasing with dataset size.([Section 5.4, p.7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1208.3719v2#page=7 "Notably, Auto-WEKA performed best on all of the 11 largest datasets; we attribute this to the fact that the risk of overﬁtting decreases with dataset size."))
- **c8** Overfitting check: Auto-WEKA optimizes on 70% of its training set and afterwards evaluates the trajectory of incumbents on the withheld 30%, reporting the Spearman rank correlation between cross-validation and held-out performance; the correlation tends to drop as the cross-validation/test gap grows (e.g. GermanCredit), but the authors caution that this weak signal gives no guarantee that a large correlation means a small gap or vice versa.([Section 5.4, p.8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1208.3719v2#page=8 "We do note, however, that this weak signal has to be used with caution: there is no guarantee that large correlation coeﬃcients yield a small gap and vice versa."))
- **c9** Stated limitation: Auto-WEKA still shows larger improvements in cross-validation performance than on test data, which the authors take as a reason to investigate more sophisticated methods for detecting and avoiding overfitting than their correlation-based approach.([Section 6, p.9](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1208.3719v2#page=9 "First, Auto-WEKA still shows larger improvements in cross-validation performance than on test data, suggesting the investigation of more sophisticated methods for detecting and avoiding overﬁtting than our simple correlation-based approach."))
- **c10** Hierarchical hyperparameter optimization (Section 2.2): given a structured (tree or DAG) space Lambda of conditional hyperparameters, the problem is lambda* in argmin_{lambda in Lambda} (1/k) sum_{i=1}^{k} L(A_lambda, D_train^(i), D_valid^(i)), the average loss over k cross-validation folds; CASH extends this by adding the choice of algorithm as a root-level hyperparameter.([Section 2.2, p.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1208.3719v2#page=2 "Given such a structured space Λ, the (hierarchical) hyperparameter optimization problem can be written as:"))
- **c11** TPE acquisition: citing Bergstra et al., the expected improvement under TPE is proportional to (gamma + g(lambda)/l(lambda) * (1 - gamma))^-1, so TPE generates many candidate configurations at random and picks the one with the smallest g(lambda)/l(lambda), where l models configurations whose loss is below the threshold c* and g the rest.([Section 3.2, p.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1208.3719v2#page=3 "TPE maximizes this expression by generating many candi-
date hyperparameter conﬁgurations at random and picking"))
