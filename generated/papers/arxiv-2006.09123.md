## 主張

出典のリンクは原論文PDFの該当ページを開く。リンクにカーソルを合わせると原文の引用が表示される。

- **c1** Contribution: the survey presents algorithms that use machine-learning predictions about the input to go beyond worst-case analysis, aiming for near-optimal performance with good predictions while recovering prediction-less worst-case behavior when predictions have large errors.([Abstract, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.09123v1#page=1 "We introduce algorithms that use predictions from machine learning applied to the input to circumvent worst-case analysis."))
- **c2** Binary search with a predicted position (classical algorithm: binary search over a sorted array): probe the predicted position h(q), then probe h(q)+2, h(q)+4, h(q)+8, ... and binary search on the bracketed interval; the cost on a query is at most 2 log of its position error eta_q = |h(q) - t(q)|, and if the queries come from a distribution, Jensen's inequality bounds the expected cost by 2 log of the expected error. The survey says even a predictor with average error O(polylog n) improves asymptotic performance, and since the error is bounded by n a bad predictor cannot do much harm.([Warm-up: Binary search, p.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.09123v1#page=2 "In particular, even a classiﬁer with an average error of O(polylog n) leads to an improvement in asymptotic performance."))
- **c3** Ski rental with a predicted number of skiing days h(d) and error eta = |h(d) - d*|: trusting the prediction costs OPT + eta but can be arbitrarily worse than the simple deterministic strategy; with a tunable lambda in [0,1] (buy on day ceil(lambda b) if h(d) > b, else on day ceil(b/lambda)) a case analysis gives a competitive ratio of at most 1 + min(1/lambda, lambda + eta/((1-lambda)OPT)), i.e. at most 1 + lambda as the error goes to 0 and never worse than 1 + 1/lambda. This deterministic worst-case bound is stated in the survey without a citation at this point; the Bibliographic Notes credit Purohit et al. (2018) with demonstrating consistency-robustness trade-offs for ski rental and scheduling (see arxiv-2407.17712).([Online algorithms: Ski Rental, p.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.09123v1#page=3 "In particular, as the error of the prediction drops to 0, the competitive ratio is no more than 1 + λ."))
- **c4** Definitions (guarantee notions): an algorithm is alpha-consistent if its competitive ratio tends to alpha as the prediction error goes to 0, and beta-robust if its competitive ratio is bounded by beta even with arbitrarily bad predictions; the survey notes a tension between the two (choice of lambda in ski rental). The Bibliographic Notes attribute the formal model including alpha-consistency and beta-robustness to Lykouris and Vassilvitskii (2018) (arxiv-1802.05399).([Model, p.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.09123v1#page=3 "Formally, we say that an algorithm is α-consistent if its competitive ratio tends to α as the error in the predictions goes to 0, and β-robust if the competitive ratio is bounded by β even with arbitrarily bad predictions."))
- **c5** Caching (classical algorithms: Belady's Furthest-In-Future rule and Marking algorithms): with a predictor h of each element's next arrival time and error eta(h) = sum over requests of |h(t) - next(t)| (normalized by opt in the bound), the PredictiveMarker variant presented in the survey (a randomized marking algorithm: evict the unmarked element predicted furthest in the future when a clean element arrives, otherwise a uniformly random unmarked element) has competitive ratio O(log(eta(h)/opt)) (Theorem 1); the proof sketch bounds expected eviction-chain lengths and uses the Diaconis-Graham inequality relating inversions to l1 distance. Attribution: the Bibliographic Notes say the analysis presented is due to Rohatgi (2020), and that Lykouris and Vassilvitskii (2018) were the first to analyze caching in this setting.([Marking Algorithms, p.8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.09123v1#page=8 "These two facts imply that the expected error of the above algorithm is O(log(η(h)/opt))."))
- **c6** Learned Bloom filters (evaluation assumption): the threshold tau is typically chosen empirically on test queries, which depends on the test queries being representative of future queries; otherwise the false positive rate may be higher than expected, so a learned Bloom filter needs different additional assumptions than a standard Bloom filter for performance statements.([Learned Bloom Filters, p.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.09123v1#page=5 "A learned Bloom ﬁlter thus requires diﬀerent additional assumptions than a standard Bloom ﬁlter in order to make statements about its performance."))
- **c7** Queue simulations (empirical, synthetic prediction noise; not a guarantee): for SPJF, PSPJF and SPRPT in a single queue with Poisson arrival rate 0.95 and exponential or Weibull service times, a job with service time x gets a prediction uniform over [(1-alpha)x, (1+alpha)x] with alpha = 0, 0.1, ..., 0.9; the survey reports that average time in system degrades gracefully with alpha and is much better than the steady-state average time in system without predictions that it states for comparison.([Scheduling Queues, p.11](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.09123v1#page=11 "A job with service time x has a predicted service time that is uniform over [(1 −α)x, (1 + α)x] for a parameter α; we try α = j/10 for integer j from 0 to 9."))
