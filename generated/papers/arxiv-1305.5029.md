## 主張

- **c1** Partition scheme: the data set is split uniformly at random into m equal-size subsets, an independent KRR estimator is computed on each subset, and the local solutions are averaged into a global predictor (divide = random partition, conquer = averaging; no learned or data-dependent partition).([Abstract, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1305.5029v2#page=1 "it randomly partitions a dataset of size N into m subsets of equal size, computes an independent kernel ridge regression estimator for each subset, then averages the local solutions into a global predictor."))
- **c2** Statistical guarantee (theory, with assumptions): the two main theorems state that, as long as the number of partitions m is not too large, the averaged estimator attains the statistical minimax rate over all estimators that use the N samples, despite the computational speed-up. The guarantee is conditional: Theorem 1 assumes f* in the RKHS with bounded conditional noise variance (Assumption B) and a moment bound on the kernel eigenfunctions (Assumption A; A' = uniform boundedness), and the rate-optimal corollaries additionally fix lambda (r/N, (1/N)^(2nu/(2nu+1)) or 1/N depending on eigendecay) and require m below an explicit threshold; Theorem 2 (f* not in H) allows a smaller m.([Abstract, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1305.5029v2#page=1 "Our two main theorems establish that despite the computational speed-up, statistical optimality is retained: as long as m is not too large, the partition-based estimator achieves the statistical minimax rate over all estimators using the set of N samples."))
- **c3** Allowed number of partitions depends on kernel eigendecay: the theory guarantees that m may grow nearly linearly in N for finite-rank and Gaussian kernels and polynomially in N for Sobolev spaces. These are the Corollary 1-3 thresholds, which hold under Assumption B and Assumption A (or A') with the corollary's specific lambda; under Assumption A the exponent depends on the number of eigenfunction moments k (the paper's summary says the thresholds grow with N when the basis functions have more than four moments).([Abstract, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1305.5029v2#page=1 "As concrete examples, our theory guarantees that the number of processors m may grow nearly linearly for ﬁnite-rank kernels and Gaussian kernels and polynomially in N for Sobolev spaces, which in turn allows for substantial reductions in computational cost."))
- **c4** What makes the averaging work: each sub-problem must be regularized as though it had all N samples, so locally it is under-regularized; this keeps each local bias small but blows up the variance, which the m-fold averaging then reduces enough to retain the optimal rate.([Introduction, p.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1305.5029v2#page=2 "Consequently, from a local point of view, each sub-problem is under-regularized."))
- **c5** Simulation check of the threshold (first-order Sobolev kernel, 1-D synthetic regression, f*(x)=min(x,1-x)): the error appears to begin increasing around log(m) of about 0.45 log(N) for reasonably large N, somewhat better than the m of order N^(1/3) threshold predicted by Corollary 2 (nu = 1); the authors read this as confirming that m can scale polynomially in N while retaining minimax optimality (an empirical check, not a proof of the empirical threshold).([Simulation studies, p.17](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1305.5029v2#page=17 "This empirical performance is somewhat better than the (1/3) thresholded predicted by Corollary 2, but it does conﬁrm that the number of partitions m can scale polynomially with N while retaining minimax optimality."))
- **c6** Real-data comparison (Million Song year prediction, Gaussian kernel): at a fixed training-time budget Fast-KRR gives the lowest test error among Fast-KRR, Nystrom subsampling and random features, but the authors state the margin over Nystrom sampling is not substantial (they add that Fast-KRR parallelizes trivially).([Real data experiments, p.19](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1305.5029v2#page=19 "As we see in Figure 3, for a ﬁxed time budget, Fast-KRR enjoys the best performance, though the margin between Fast-KRR and Nystr¨om sampling is not substantial."))
- **c7** Stated open problem: the guarantees use a specific choice of the regularization lambda, but the authors note the optimal lambda may not be known a priori in practice, and say it is unclear how to choose it adaptively (e.g. by cross-validation or unbiased risk estimation) in the distributed setting where each local estimate depends only on its own subset.([Main results and their consequences, p.8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1305.5029v2#page=8 "Cross-validation or other types of unbiased risk estimation are natural choices, however, it is at this point unclear how to achieve such behavior in the distributed setting we study"))
- **c8** KRR estimator (Eq. 2): fhat = argmin over f in H of (1/N) sum_{i=1}^{N} (f(x_i) - y_i)^2 + lambda ||f||_H^2 with lambda > 0, i.e. least-squares loss plus a squared Hilbert-norm penalty; the paper calls it a natural generalization of ordinary ridge regression to the non-parametric setting.([Section 2.2, p.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1305.5029v2#page=4 "It is a natural generalization of the ordinary ridge regression estimate [13] to the non-parametric setting."))
- **c9** Representer theorem: any solution of the KRR program lies in the linear span of the kernel functions K(., x_i), so computing the estimate reduces to an N-dimensional quadratic program involving the N^2 kernel-matrix entries.([Section 2.2, p.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1305.5029v2#page=4 "By the representer theorem for reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces [31], any solution to the KRR program (2) must belong to the linear span of the kernel functions {K(·, xi), i = 1, . . . , N}."))
