## 主張

出典のリンクは原論文PDFの該当ページを開く。リンクにカーソルを合わせると原文の引用が表示される。

- **c1** Motivation (citing Creutz, 1988): the cost of HMC per independent sample from a D-dimensional target is roughly O(D^(5/4)), in contrast with O(D^2) for random-walk Metropolis. The price is that HMC needs the gradient of the log-posterior and the user must set a step size and a number of steps, and a poor choice of either causes a dramatic drop in efficiency.([Introduction, p.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1111.4246v1#page=2 "which stands in sharp contrast with the O(D2) cost of random-walk Metropolis (Creutz, 1988)"))
- **c2** The naive rule of running leapfrog steps until the trajectory starts moving back towards the starting point does not guarantee time reversibility and therefore is not guaranteed to converge to the correct distribution; NUTS instead uses a recursive doubling procedure (reminiscent of Neal's slice-sampling doubling) and must exclude from the candidate set any states that could not have generated the same tree.([Section 3.1, p.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1111.4246v1#page=5 "Unfortunately this algorithm does not guarantee time reversibility, and is therefore not guaranteed to converge to the correct distribution."))
- **c3** Besides the U-turn rule, NUTS stops doubling when the simulation error becomes extremely large (the log joint density of a leaf falls more than Delta_max below the slice variable); the authors note this may happen if the step size is too large or if hard constraints make the log density go to minus infinity in some regions.([Section 3.1.1, p.9](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1111.4246v1#page=9 "We also want to stop expanding B if the error in the simulation becomes extremely large, indicating that any states discovered by continuing the simulation longer are likely to have astronomically low probability."))
- **c4** Step-size adaptation: Robbins-Monro diminishing step sizes give disproportionate weight to early iterations, so the authors adapt dual averaging (with shrinkage target mu, free parameters gamma, t0 and kappa) to drive an acceptance statistic towards a target delta, with x = log(epsilon); for NUTS the statistic is the average HMC acceptance probability of the states explored during the final doubling. In Algorithms 5 and 6 adaptation runs for M_adapt iterations and the step size is then fixed.([Section 3.2, p.15](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1111.4246v1#page=15 "However, the diminishing step sizes of Robbins-Monro give disproportionate weight to the early iterations, which is the opposite of what we want."))
- **c5** The dual-averaging parameters (gamma = 0.05, t0 = 10, kappa = 0.75) were chosen by trying a few settings by hand on the stochastic volatility model; the authors say they seem to work consistently well for all models tested but that it is entirely possible they may not work as well for other samplers or other statistics.([Section 3.2, p.16](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1111.4246v1#page=16 "Better results might be obtained with further tweaking, but these default parameters seem to work consistently well for both NUTS and HMC for all of the models that we tested."))
- **c6** Evaluation protocol: four targets (a 250-dimensional correlated multivariate normal, Bayesian logistic regression and hierarchical logistic regression on the German credit data, and a 3001-dimensional stochastic volatility posterior); each run has 2000 iterations with step-size adaptation in the first 1000, HMC with 10 simulation lengths and several delta targets, 10 seeds per setting. Efficiency is ESS per gradient evaluation, taking the minimum over dimensions of the ESS for the mean and the second central moment, with reference mean and variance from a separate 50,000-iteration NUTS run (delta = 0.5).([Section 4, p.19](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1111.4246v1#page=19 "We measure the eﬃciency of each algorithm in terms of eﬀective sample size (ESS) normalized by the number of gradient evaluations used by each algorithm."))
- **c7** Recommended targets: HMC's best performance seems to occur around delta = 0.65 and NUTS's around delta = 0.6, without strong dependence on delta within [0.45, 0.65] for NUTS, so the authors suggest 0.6 as a reasonable default for NUTS.([Section 4.4, p.25](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1111.4246v1#page=25 "δ = 0.6 therefore seems like a reasonable default value for NUTS."))
- **c8** Comparison with HMC (text statement from Figure 6): on the two logistic regression problems NUTS is about as efficient as HMC, on the multivariate normal and stochastic volatility problems NUTS with delta = 0.6 outperforms HMC's best ESS by about a factor of three; the best HMC simulation lengths varied by about a factor of 100 across targets, so the authors say the results suggest NUTS is at least as efficient as HMC even ignoring HMC's tuning runs.([Section 4.4, p.25](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1111.4246v1#page=25 "The results in Figure 6 suggest that NUTS can generate samples at least as eﬃciently as HMC, even discounting the cost of any preliminary runs needed to tune HMC’s simulation length."))
- **c9** Scope limits: NUTS was compared only with basic HMC (not its extensions) and only with an identity mass matrix; like HMC it can only resample unconstrained continuous variables with respect to which the target is differentiable almost everywhere, so discrete variables must be summed out or handled by other algorithms such as Gibbs sampling.([Section 5 (Discussion), p.27](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1111.4246v1#page=27 "Like HMC, NUTS can only be used to resample unconstrained continuous-valued variables with respect to which the target distribution is diﬀerentiable almost everywhere."))
