## 主張

- **c1** Background (citing prior empirical work): MDI is known to favour variables with many categories (citing Strobl et al., 2007; Nicodemus, 2011); even with equal numbers of categories it shows empirical bias towards variables with a high-frequency category (citing Nicodemus, 2011; Boulesteix et al., 2011), and it is biased in the presence of correlated features (citing Nicodemus and Malley, 2009).([Introduction, p.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.04295v3#page=2 "MDI is known to favor variables with many categories [see, e.g., Strobl et al., 2007, Nicodemus, 2011]."))
- **c2** Background (citing Strobl et al., 2008 and Strobl and Zeileis, 2008): MDA (permutation importance) seems to exhibit less bias than MDI but tends to overestimate correlated features; its scaled version (the default in the R package randomForest) depends on the sample size and on the number of trees.([Introduction, p.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.04295v3#page=2 "On the other hand, MDA seems to exhibit less bias than MDI but tends to overestimate correlated features Strobl et al. [2008]."))
- **c3** Scope: the analysis is confined to regression with bounded continuous inputs (X in [0,1]^d); the author states that extending it to classification is not straightforward because the main result relies on the additive regression framework.([Section 2 (Remark 1), p.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.04295v3#page=4 "Extending our work to the classiﬁcation setting is not straightforward, since our main result relies on the additive regression framework which does not have any equivalent in classiﬁcation."))
- **c4** Fully grown trees: for trees with one observation per leaf the sum of empirical MDI tends to V[m(X)] + sigma^2, i.e. it also contains the noise, so MDI of some variables is higher than expected; when the noise variance is larger than that of the regression function this can induce a very important bias. The author concludes that MDI from a fully grown tree must never be used for interpretation, whereas MDI from trees of fixed, large enough depth estimates the population MDI (Theorem 1).([Section 3, p.9](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.04295v3#page=9 "The noise, when having a larger variance than the regression function, can induce a very important bias in the MDI by overestimating the importance of some variables."))
- **c5** Additive model (Theorem 3): under Model 3 (Y = sum_j m_j(X_j) + Gaussian noise, m_j continuous, X uniform on [0,1]^d, hence independent inputs), the empirical MDI of each variable computed with a truncated (depth-k) CART tree converges to V[m_j(X_j)]; since forest MDI is the average of tree MDI, the result also holds for random forests. The author stresses that MDI must not be computed with fully grown trees, which would give positively biased MDI.([Section 4, p.12](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.04295v3#page=12 "Theorem 3 is the ﬁrst result highlighting that empirical MDI computed with CART procedure converge to reasonable values that can be used for variable selection, in the framework of Model 3."))
- **c6** Citing Gregorutti et al. (2017) for the population MDA, the author notes that in additive models with independent features MDI and MDA target the same quantity (up to a factor 2), so both can then be used to rank features and select variables.([Section 4, p.12](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.04295v3#page=12 "Thus, in the particular context of additive models with independent features, both MDI and MDA target the same quantity, which is the natural way to decompose the total variance."))
- **c7** Interactions (multiplicative Model 5): different theoretical CART trees can output different MDI for the same variable (Lemma 2), and the variable split in the second step can receive larger importance than the one split first; the author suggests that averaging MDI over many randomized trees, as in random forests, is a way to circumvent this, since a single tree cannot give the symmetric importances one can hope for.([Section 5, p.16](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.04295v3#page=16 "Since MDI are usually used to rank and select variables, the fact that each tree can output a diﬀerent MDI, and therefore a diﬀerent variable ranking, is a major drawback for employing this measure in presence of interactions."))
- **c8** Dependent inputs (Model 6: Y = X1 + X2 + alpha X3 + noise with (X1, X2) positively correlated on diagonal squares, X3 independent): CART splits favour the positively correlated variables (Proposition 4), only the sum of their MDI is identified (Theorem 6) and it exceeds V[X1] + V[X2], and two theoretical trees can split it very differently between X1 and X2 (Lemma 4). The author notes the opposite effect would hold for negative correlation or coefficients of opposite signs, and recommends averaging over many shallow trees rather than using a single tree.([Section 6, p.19](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.04295v3#page=19 "Therefore, the group of variables X(1) and X(2) has a larger importance because of their positive correlation."))
- **c9** In regression, the CART-split criterion can be rewritten as the difference in impurity between a cell and its two children, where the impurity is defined as the variance of the output.([CART construction and empirical MDI, p.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.04295v3#page=5 "where the impurity is simply deﬁned as the variance of the output in regression"))
- **c10** Definition of MDI (Eq. 3): the MDI of variable X(j) computed via a tree is the sum, over all cells split along X(j), of the fraction of observations falling into the cell times the value of the CART-split criterion (impurity decrease) of that split, i.e. the weighted decrease in impurity related to splits along X(j).([CART construction and empirical MDI, p.6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.04295v3#page=6 "In other words, the MDI of X(j) computes the weighted decrease in impurity related to splits along the variable X(j)."))
