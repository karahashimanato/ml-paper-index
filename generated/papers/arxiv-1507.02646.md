## 主張

出典のリンクは原論文PDFの該当ページを開く。リンクにカーソルを合わせると原文の引用が表示される。

- **c1** Method: the M largest importance ratios, M = floor(min(0.2S, 3 sqrt(S))), are used to fit a generalized Pareto distribution (k and sigma estimated with the method of Zhang and Stephens (2009) plus an additional weak prior, Appendix G); these ratios are replaced by the inverse CDF evaluated at (z - 1/2)/M, z = 1..M (an approximation of expected order statistics), truncated at the largest raw ratio.([Section 2.2, p.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1507.02646v9#page=4 "We propose a new method to stabilize the importance weights by replacing the M largest weights above the threshold u by a set of well-spaced values that are consistent with the tails of the importance distribution,"))
- **c2** Assumption: the method assumes, as a working assumption that usually cannot be verified, that the distribution of the ratios lies in the domain of attraction of an extremal distribution (citing Pickands (1975) that most textbook continuous distributions do); for finite S, k-hat can be read as saying the sample of ratios is not statistically distinguishable from one with tail index k-hat.([Section 2.1, p.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1507.02646v9#page=4 "Although in most cases we cannot verify that the distribution of rs lies in the domain of attraction for an extremal distribution, we will use this as a working assumption for building our method."))
- **c3** Warning rule in the algorithm: if the estimated shape k-hat exceeds min(1 - 1/log10(S), 0.7), PSIS reports that the importance sampling estimates are likely to be unstable or have high bias; for S > 2000 this threshold is 0.7.([Section 2.2 (Algorithm 1), p.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1507.02646v9#page=5 "For S > 2000 this threshold is 0.7."))
- **c4** Why not 0.5: trusting importance sampling only when k-hat < 0.5 (finite variance, CLT holds) would be simple, but because the theory shows PSIS is valid for any integrable density, the authors consider requiring k-hat < 0.5 unnecessarily stringent; extensive experiments show PSIS is reliable (low bias, low variance) when k-hat < 0.7 for typical moderate sample sizes (approximately S > 2000).([Section 3, p.6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1507.02646v9#page=6 "This suggests that requiring the estimated shape parameter be less than 0.5 would be unnecessarily stringent."))
- **c5** Justification of 0.7: by numerical analysis of truncated Pareto means, when k < 0.7 the variance dominates the PSIS RMSE (so the variance-based MCSE works even for k > 0.5); when k > 0.7 bias dominates regardless of S and the variance-based MCSE starts to fail, so accuracy is harder to diagnose even if S is increased.([Section 3.2.3, p.10](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1507.02646v9#page=10 "When k > 0.7, independently of S the effect of bias dominates and the variance based MCSE starts to fail (also demonstrated by the examples)."))
- **c6** Sample-size-specific threshold 1 - 1/log10(S) (rule of thumb for S >= 10): sample sizes 1000, 2000, 4000 and 10 000 give thresholds 0.67, 0.7, 0.72 and 0.75, all approximated by 0.7; S = 100 gives 0.5 and S = 100 000 gives 0.8.([Section 3.2.4, p.11](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1507.02646v9#page=11 "For a much smaller sample size 100, the threshold would be 0.5, and for a much bigger sample size 100 000 the threshold would be 0.8."))
- **c7** Summary recommendations: if k-hat < 1 - 1/log10(S) the PSIS estimate is expected to be accurate; if k-hat < min(1 - 1/log10(S), 0.7) the PSIS MCSE estimate is expected to be reliable; if k-hat > 0.7 it quickly becomes too expensive to get an accurate estimate; if k-hat > 1 the mean is expected not to exist and any estimate of it is invalid.([Section 6, p.25](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1507.02646v9#page=25 "If ˆk > 1, it is expected that the mean does not exist and any estimate for the mean is invalid."))
- **c8** k-hat is a pre-asymptotic diagnostic: in Example 2 (D-dimensional standard normal target, Student-t proposal with 7 degrees of freedom, S = 10 000, D from 1 to 1024) the ratios are always bounded with finite variance, yet the finite-sample behavior becomes indistinguishable from the infinite-variance case as D grows, and k-hat diagnoses the collapse of effective sample size and convergence rate.([Section 3.3, p.15](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1507.02646v9#page=15 "Although the importance ratios are always bounded and the variance is finite, the finite sample behavior is indistinguishable from the infinite variance case when the number of dimensions grows large enough."))
- **c9** Known blind spot: one could construct an artificial example (citing a discrete-distribution example of Chatterjee and Diaconis, 2018) in which the tail looks thin (low k-hat) for small S with high probability while the true tail is thick; the authors report not observing this, except for very small S, in their simulations and applications.([Section 3.2.9, p.15](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1507.02646v9#page=15 "It would be possible to construct also an artificial example where the tail would look thin (low Pareto ˆk) for small S with large probability, while the true tail is thick (high Pareto ˆk)."))
- **c10** Self-normalized importance sampling (Eq. 1): E_p[h] is estimated by sum_s r_s h(theta_s) / sum_s r_s with importance ratios r_s = p(theta_s)/g(theta_s), using draws from a proposal g; when g poorly approximates the target, the ratios can have a heavy right tail and the estimate can be unstable, sometimes with infinite variance.([Introduction, p.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1507.02646v9#page=2 "When the proposal distribution is a poor approximation to the target distribution, the distribution of importance ratios can have a heavy right tail."))
