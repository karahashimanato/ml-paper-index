## 主張

出典のリンクは原論文PDFの該当ページを開く。リンクにカーソルを合わせると原文の引用が表示される。

- **c1** Main thesis: forests improve upon trees by three distinct, usually entangled mechanisms: smoothing reduces variance due to outcome noise, reduces variability in the quality of the learned function given fixed inputs, and reduces potential bias by enriching the hypothesis space.([Abstract, p.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.01502v1#page=1 "Instead, we show that forests can improve upon trees by three distinct mechanisms that are usually implicitly entangled."))
- **c2** Argument for interpolating (full-depth) ensembles: each tree acts as a 1-NN estimator at both training and test inputs, but different randomized trees can place an unseen input in leaves with different training points, so the forest is smoother at test inputs than at training inputs and smoother than individual trees there.([Section 2.5, p.9](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.01502v1#page=9 "Therefore, interpolating forests should be expected to (i) be smoother when issuing predictions for testing examples than for training examples and (ii) be smoother than individual trees at previously unseen test inputs"))
- **c3** Evaluation setup: the 'MARSadd' simulation (d = 5 uniform features, n_train = n_test = 500, sigma = 1 by default), averaged over 10 seeds; Appendix C replicates analyses on 4 datasets from Grinsztajn et al. (2022) subsampled to 2000 train/test points; trees, forests and boosting use scikit-learn RandomForestRegressor / GradientBoostingRegressor. Results are shown as figures.([Section 3 (Experimental setup), p.10](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.01502v1#page=10 "All experiments are averaged over 10 replications with different random seeds and shaded areas in plots represent 95% confidence intervals."))
- **c4** Spiked-smooth behaviour (Wyner et al. 2017) is quantified as a gap between train-time and test-time effective parameters; it also appears in non-interpolating forests (limited number of leaves) but is much more pronounced for interpolating trees, leading the authors to conjecture it appears whenever there is some overfitting.([Section 3.1.2, p.12](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.01502v1#page=12 "We thus conjecture that spiked-smooth behavior appears whenever there is some degree of overfitting to the training data."))
- **c5** For ensembles of 50 interpolating trees, adding randomness by lowering the fraction m of features per split appears to improve generalization at first, then hurts once tree construction becomes too random (a U-shape, analogous to k in k-NN); adding more trees never hurt generalization error in their experiments.([Section 3.1.1, p.11](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.01502v1#page=11 "We observe that in-troducing more randomness (by decreasing m) appears to improve generalization initially, until a turning point is reached when additional randomness hurts performance – this is when tree construction becomes too random."))
- **c6** The covariance-based degrees of freedom used by Mentch and Zhou (2020) cannot fully explain forest-vs-tree performance differences (e.g. it is constant across ensemble sizes); the authors argue Wyner et al.'s spiked-smooth explanation is a special case of Mentch and Zhou's randomization-as-regularization explanation.([Section 3, p.9](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.01502v1#page=9 "We show that their chosen measure for degrees of freedom cannot fully explain the success of forests relative to trees"))
- **c7** Trees and forests (built from i.i.d. Theta) have the same statistical bias, so classically all gains are attributed to variance reduction; the authors argue this notion misses differences in what trees and forests can represent, and propose distinguishing them by representation bias (forests' hypothesis class contains that of trees).([Section 4.1.3, p.19](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.01502v1#page=19 "We therefore argue that distinguishing trees and forests by their representation bias can be more meaningful."))
- **c8** In the noiseless setting (sigma = 0), bagging full-depth trees worsened in-sample error relative to interpolating trees, while for unseen test inputs forests improved over individual trees even without label noise (shown in Figure 10).([Section 4.2.1, p.21](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.01502v1#page=21 "when given previously unseen test examples, Panel (A) of Fig. 10 highlights that forest predictions can improve upon individual trees even in the absence of any label noise (σ = 0)!"))
- **c9** Protocol caveat for the RepBias/ModVar analysis: 50 re-seeded versions of each model are trained on a fixed noiseless train-test set (delta = 0.1) and the best one is selected using oracle knowledge of the test outcomes as a proxy for representation bias.([Section 4.2.2, p.23](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.01502v1#page=23 "Among these 50 models, we measure performance on the test set and identify the best-performing model"))
- **c10** Practical takeaway: bootstrapping mainly affects in-sample error (ability to overfit labels at observed inputs), whereas randomness in feature selection matters much more for out-of-sample performance, especially when trees fit the training data perfectly; too much randomness can hurt.([Conclusion (Practical takeaways), p.25](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.01502v1#page=25 "Randomness in feature selection, especially when the training data can be fit perfectly, is much more important for out-of-sample prediction performance."))
