## 主張

出典のリンクは原論文PDFの該当ページを開く。リンクにカーソルを合わせると原文の引用が表示される。

- **c1** Architecture: SAINT projects all features into a combined dense space and passes them as tokens through a transformer encoder with self-attention over the features of a sample and a proposed intersample attention that relates a row to other rows; the authors describe intersample attention as akin to nearest-neighbor classification with a distance metric learned end-to-end, and say it lets SAINT borrow features from similar samples in the batch when a feature is missing or noisy.([Introduction, p.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.01342v1#page=2 "Intersample attention is akin to a nearest-neighbor classiﬁcation, where the distance metric is learned end-to-end rather than ﬁxed."))
- **c2** Embedding of continuous features: unlike TabTransformer, which embeds only categorical features (continuous features bypass the transformer, which the authors say loses information about categorical-continuous correlations), SAINT projects each continuous feature with its own single fully-connected layer with ReLU; columns are embedded independently. In a side experiment that keeps TabTransformer's architecture and training hyperparameters fixed, only embedding its continuous features this way raised its average AUROC from 89.38 to 91.72.([Self-Attention and Intersample Attention Transformer (SAINT): Encoding the Data, p.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.01342v1#page=4 "we use a separate single fully-connected layer with a ReLU nonlinearity for each continuous feature"))
- **c3** Pre-training: augmented views are generated with CutMix in the raw input space and mixup in the embedding space; the loss is an InfoNCE contrastive loss between projection-head outputs of clean and augmented views plus a denoising loss reconstructing each feature (cross-entropy or MSE) with lambda_pt and temperature described as tuned on validation data. The authors state that, to the best of their knowledge, they are the first to adopt contrastive learning for tabular data.([Related Work, p.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.01342v1#page=3 "In this paper, to the best of our knowledge, we are the ﬁrst to adopt contrastive learning for tabular data."))
- **c4** Benchmark: 16 public datasets from UCI or AutoML (14 binary, 2 multi-class: Volkert and MNIST), chosen because they were previously used to evaluate competing methods (TabNet, TabTransformer, VIME); sizes range from 200 to 495,141 samples and 8 to 784 features. AUROC is the main metric for binary tasks and accuracy for the two multi-class datasets.([Experimental Evaluation: Datasets, p.6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.01342v1#page=6 "These datasets were chosen because they were previously used to evaluate competing methods [1, 18, 49]."))
- **c5** Protocol: all models (including pre-training runs) are trained with AdamW (learning rate 0.0001, batch size 256 except for wide datasets); data split as stated into 65%, 15% and 25% for training, validation and test; each reported number is the mean of 5 trials with different seeds. Baseline results (logistic regression, random forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost, MLP, VIME, TabNet, TabTransformer) are quoted from the original papers when possible and reproduced otherwise. No description of how the GBDT baselines' hyperparameters were tuned was found in the text (searched for tun, hyperparam, hyper-param, default, grid).([Table 2 caption, p.7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.01342v1#page=7 "Baseline results are quoted from original papers when possible (denoted with *) and reproduced otherwise."))
- **c6** Supervised results (within this paper): on 13 of the 16 datasets one of the three SAINT variants (SAINT, SAINT-s with self-attention only, SAINT-i with intersample attention only) outperforms all baselines, including the boosted-tree libraries, and on the remaining 3 it is very close to the best; on average each variant is reported to beat all baselines by a significant margin. Note the count takes the best of three variants per dataset.([Results: Supervised setting, p.7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.01342v1#page=7 "In 13 out of 16 datasets, one of the SAINT variants outperforms all baseline models."))
- **c7** Semi-supervised results: with 50, 200 and 500 labeled points (rest unlabeled), the pre-trained SAINT model performs best in all cases; when all training samples are labeled, pre-training does not contribute appreciably.([Results: Semi-supervised setting, p.8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.01342v1#page=8 "Interestingly, we note that when all the training data samples are labeled, pre-training does not contribute appreciably, hence the results with and without pre-training are fairly close."))
- **c8** Interpretability: the authors say attention gives some interpretability (with one transformer stage the maps reveal which features and data points are used); the evidence is visualizations on MNIST treated as tabular data (self-attention focusing on foreground pixels; sparse intersample attention on a few points, which they hypothesize are hard examples) and Volkert, where intersample attention is much denser.([Interpreting attention, p.8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.01342v1#page=8 "One advantage of using transformer-based models is that attention comes with some interpretability, in contrast, MLPs are hard to interpret."))
- **c9** Stated limitation: real-world datasets may be highly noisy or imbalanced, and SAINT was tuned for the settings in which it was tested, so the authors caution practitioners against assuming that what works on these benchmarks will work in their own setting.([Discussion, Limitations, and Impact, p.10](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.01342v1#page=10 "Moreover, we have tuned SAINT for the settings in which we test it."))
