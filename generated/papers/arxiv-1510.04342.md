## 主張

出典のリンクは原論文PDFの該当ページを開く。リンクにカーソルを合わせると原文の引用が表示される。

- **c1** Honesty: the results require each tree to be honest, i.e. a training example's response is used either to place splits or to estimate the within-leaf effect, not both. Two honest constructions: double-sample trees (the subsample is split into halves I and J; J places splits, I estimates leaf effects; splits maximise the variance of the effect estimates following Athey and Imbens, 2016) and propensity trees (splits placed by a classification tree on the treatment W only).([Section 2.4 (Honest Trees and Forests), p.8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1510.04342v4#page=8 "a tree is honest if, for each training example i, it only uses the response Yi to estimate the within-leaf treatment eﬀect τ using (5) or to decide where to place the splits, but not both."))
- **c2** Cost of sample splitting: the authors argue honesty via double-sample trees does not waste data in the forest because the I/J split is re-randomized for every subsample, so each point is used for both structure and estimation across trees; they report that double-sample trees can also improve mean-squared error over standard random forests in practice.([Section 2.4 (Honest Trees and Forests), p.8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1510.04342v4#page=8 "However, in our case, the forest subampling mechanism enables us to achieve honesty without wasting any data in this sense, because we re-randomize the I/J -data splits over each subsample."))
- **c3** Assumptions for causal inference: unconfoundedness (potential outcomes independent of W given X), Lipschitz continuity of E[Y(0)|X=x] and E[Y(1)|X=x], and overlap (epsilon < P(W=1|X=x) < 1 - epsilon); under these and the forest conditions, Theorem 11 states causal forest predictions are consistent, asymptotically Gaussian and centered, with variance consistently estimated by the infinitesimal jackknife (assuming enough trees that Monte Carlo variability does not matter).([Section 2.3, p.7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1510.04342v4#page=7 "This condition eﬀectively guarantees that, for large enough n, there will be enough treatment and control units near any test point x for local methods to work."))
- **c4** Conditions of Theorem 1 (regression forests): i.i.d. data with X uniform on [0,1]^d (a density bounded away from 0 and infinity also suffices), Lipschitz mu(x) and E[Y^2|X=x], positive conditional variance and a 2+delta moment bound; trees honest, alpha-regular with alpha <= 0.2 (leaves with k to 2k-1 observations), symmetric and random-split (each feature split with probability at least pi/d); subsample size s ~ n^beta with beta_min < beta < 1, without replacement. If s grows slower, the forest is still asymptotically normal but may be asymptotically biased.([Section 3, p.11](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1510.04342v4#page=11 "If the subsample size grows slower than this, the forest will still be asymptotically normal, but the forest may be asymptotically biased."))
- **c5** Why honesty: in a randomized-trial example with rare outliers, adaptive (non-honest) CART forests with leaf size 1 were biased at a corner of the feature space, with bias increasing with n, while honest forests were unbiased; the authors state that pointwise centered asymptotic Gaussianity appears to require honesty and that their theorems are not valid for adaptive forests.([Appendix B, p.31](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1510.04342v4#page=31 "However, if we want pointwise centered asymptotic Gaussianity results, then honesty appears to be necessary."))
- **c6** Honest vs adaptive mean-squared error (setting of Table 2, d = 8): at their best both perform comparably, but adaptive forests with the usual minimum leaf size k = 1 do extremely badly and are very sensitive to k, whereas honest forests perform well over a wide range of k.([Appendix B.1, p.32](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1510.04342v4#page=32 "Thus, it appears that accurately tuning adaptive forests in this setting may present a challenge and, overall, a practitioner may prefer honest forests even based on their mean-squared error characteristics alone."))
- **c7** Simulation setup: X ~ U([0,1]^d), unconfoundedness respected, homoscedastic Gaussian noise; baseline k-NN matching; metrics are MSE for tau(X) at random test points and coverage with target 0.95. Setup 1 (no effect, confounding via e(x) and m(x)): n = 500, d from 2 to 30, propensity trees, B = 1000, s = 50. Setup 2 (heterogeneous effect in a randomized design): n = 5000, d from 2 to 8, double-sample trees, s = 2500, B = 2000. Setup 3 (sharper effect peak): n = 10000, s = 2000, B = 10000. Implemented in R (causalTree, randomForestCI, FNN).([Simulation Experiments, p.21](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1510.04342v4#page=21 "All our trees had a minimum leaf size of k = 1."))
- **c8** Stated limitations: in setup 3 coverage struggles, which the authors attribute largely to bias dominating variance as d grows; whether to use double-sample or propensity forests was chosen manually per setting, and automatic splitting rules and a principled rule for choosing s are left open.([Simulation Experiments (Results), p.24](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1510.04342v4#page=24 "A limitation of the present simulation study is that we manually chose whether to use double-sample forests or propensity forests, depending on which procedure seemed more appropriate in each problem setting."))
